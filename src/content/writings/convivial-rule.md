## Designing Rules: Creating, Reviewing, and Letting Go

In organizations, there are sometimes rules that no one can explain the reason for.

"We've always done it this way," "Just in case something goes wrong," or "It's the rule, so please follow it." When told this, it is hard to question further. There might have been a pressing reason when the rule was first created. However, even as circumstances change, only the rule remains, and before we know it, following the rule itself becomes the job.

Rules are originally tools to solve problems. We want to stabilize quality. We want to prevent accidents. We want people from different backgrounds to cooperate. These objectives come first, and rules are created as a means to achieve them.

However, when rules are used for a long time, the means and ends can swap places. Instead of rules helping human judgment, humans begin to act simply to maintain the rules.

Are we using rules, or are we being ruled by them? In this article, I would like to explore rules through three actions: "creating," "reviewing," and "letting go."

## What Do Rules Solve?

In *The Design of Rules: Maximizing Organizational Performance*, Yuki Anzai and Tasuku Mizuno divide organizational rules into "rules of control" and "rules of creation."

Rules of control are used to prevent problems and maintain order. They standardize work procedures and keep quality consistent through planning and management. Decision-making is centralized at higher levels, while frontline workers handle designated scopes. This is indispensable in areas where the cost of failure is high, such as compliance, accounting, information security, and safety management.

Rules of creation are used to face problems for which the correct answer is not yet known. Rather than trying to avoid failure completely, they focus on learning from small experiments. Roles and authority are delegated to the front lines, and judgments are updated through dialogue. The goal is to foster psychological safety and enable the organization to adapt its actions to changes in the external environment.

If rules of control clarify "what not to do," rules of creation clarify "what is okay to try."

For example, requiring multiple approvals every time a new project is launched might reduce major failures. In exchange, however, it also reduces the number of trials. With rules of creation, you define a boundary within which losses can be limited, allowing frontline staff to experiment at their own discretion within that range. The results are shared, and even failures are used as input for the next decision.

It is important not to overlook that rules of creation are still rules. Even in organizations that seem completely free, there are agreements such as "listen to differing opinions," "test small," "do not hide failures," and "the decision-maker must reflect on the results." Instead of specifying detailed actions, they define how decisions should be made.

This is not a matter of which is superior, control or creation. The nature of the problems they address is different. Control is suited for work where reproducibility of procedures is crucial. Creation is suited for work with high uncertainty that requires adapting to change.

The problem arises when we try to solve every single issue with rules of control. Every time something happens, we add more approvers, require more reporting items, and ban exceptions. The organization might appear to become safer little by little, but at the same time, it turns into a place where people no longer need to think for themselves.

## Rules Becoming the Organizational Norm

Edgar Schein viewed organizational culture through three levels: visible artifacts and behaviors, espoused values, and underlying basic assumptions that are usually unconscious.

Rules initially appear as visible artifacts, such as regulations and approval workflows. They are accompanied by justifications like "maintaining quality" or "making fair judgments." As these rules are repeatedly applied, assumptions like "acting without a manager's approval is dangerous" or "allowing exceptions disrupts order" seep deep into the organization.

Once it reaches this stage, reviewing a rule is no longer a simple procedural change. This is because questioning the rule is perceived as an act of denying the organization's core values.

Sometimes, there is a gap between stated goals and actual behavior. An organization might champion "welcoming challenges" while maintaining multiple levels of approval to avoid failure. It might say "we trust the front lines" while demanding detailed reports. Even if the espoused values remain, daily actions are driven in a different direction by the rules.

Ivan Illich described the turning point where tools stop supporting human capabilities and instead begin forcing humans to adapt to the tools as the "second watershed." Something similar happens with rules. Rules that were supposed to assist in making decisions end up stripping away the very opportunity to make decisions.

Being ruled by rules does not just mean having many prohibitions. It means not knowing what a rule is for, not being able to choose other methods, and not being able to propose changes even when something feels wrong. When people lose these three things—purpose, options, and agency—they can no longer choose their relationship with the rules.

## Designing Rules with the Intent to Redesign Them

It is difficult to create a perfect rule from the start. When designing a rule, we cannot know what the future environment will look like or how people will react. Even so, we build systems assuming that "these conditions will continue" and "if we put this rule in place, people will act this way."

The concept of "adaptive institutional design," introduced by Takahiro Ezaki in *Rule Design: Unraveling with Mathematical Model Thinking*, starts from the premise that we cannot perfectly predict or control the world. What matters is not making accurate predictions, but being able to change the system when those predictions turn out to be wrong.

In adaptive institutional design, you first define the goals and map out the possible options. Instead of immediately jumping to prohibitions or mandates, you consider what you want to achieve and whether there are non-rule alternatives to get there.

Next, you construct the basic rules. You decide who they apply to, which decisions should be standardized, and where the front lines should take over. At this stage, you also write down the assumptions under which these basic rules are expected to function.

For example, suppose you are creating rules for using generative AI services within a company. A rule like "only use approved services" assumes that the approval process is fast enough and that the person in charge understands the services the front lines actually need. If new services emerge one after another and reviews take several months, that assumption falls apart. The more strictly people follow the rule, the more work grinds to a halt, potentially driving users to shadow IT (unofficial channels).

Therefore, you should also decide in advance how to respond when assumptions fail. If the review period exceeds a certain limit, switch to a simplified review. If exception requests increase, revise the approval criteria. If a major incident occurs, temporarily narrow the scope of use. Once the situation stabilizes, return the decision-making power to the front lines.

Finally, you decide what to observe and which changes will serve as triggers for updates. Simply writing "review periodically" usually means it will never be reviewed. You need to specify "which conditions must change, by how much, for whom to change which rule."

These rules for changing rules are called "meta-rules." You establish in advance the timing of reviews, who can propose changes, how exceptions are handled, and the conditions for suspension or abolition.

It is easy to understand this when thinking about software. No one would dare run a system that only writes happy-path code without any monitoring, exception handling, or rollback mechanisms. Yet, when it comes to organizational rules, we design them under the assumption that they will run smoothly forever. Systems that deal with humans and society are precisely the ones that need mechanisms to detect failures and roll back.

Acknowledging a rule's failure is slightly different from admitting the designer's failure. When the environment changes, rules that were once correct no longer fit. Being able to update them is not a weakness of the system; rather, it is a strength that allows it to withstand change.

## Rules That Support Human Agency

When reviewing rules, using compliance rate as the sole metric of success leads to poor decisions. Placing strict monitoring and penalties might increase superficial compliance. However, if people stop thinking, hide problems, and only act within the prescribed boundaries, the organization loses other vital capabilities.

The concepts of autonomy, competence, and relatedness from self-determination theory provide clues for thinking about how rules affect humans.

Autonomy does not mean being able to decide everything by oneself. It means understanding the purpose and choosing actions with conviction. Competence refers to feeling that one's judgments and actions lead to results and that one is growing. Relatedness is the sense of being accepted by others and cooperating within a framework of trust.

Good rules do not completely strip away these three elements. Even if a rule successfully forces compliance, if the people involved cannot understand the reason, cannot exercise their abilities, and end up monitoring each other, that rule is weakening human activity.

Convivial rules are not programs designed to manipulate humans at will. They are scaffolds for people to make judgments and collaborate together. They leave room for participation, dissent, adaptation to circumstances, and letting go once their role is finished.

## A Company with No Approvals and No Set Vacation Days

You don't need your manager's approval before spending company money. The company doesn't specify a set number of vacation days. Even critical decisions aren't necessarily made by managers.

Wouldn't such a company fall into chaos?

Netflix has eliminated many prior approvals and detailed regulations, including those for expenses and vacation. This philosophy is introduced in *No Rules: Netflix and the Culture of Reinvention* by Reed Hastings and Erin Meyer.

However, Netflix has not let go of responsibility. The shared principle regarding expenses is brief:

> Act in Netflix’s best interest.

There is a certain clarity to a fixed budget. Within that limit, employees can spend with peace of mind. However, when the budget amount becomes the standard for spending, decisions are made based on "is it under the limit?" rather than "is it truly necessary?" Sometimes, spending the remaining budget simply to use it up becomes the goal.

Under Netflix's principle, simply staying within a dollar amount does not count as making a decision. The individual must think about whether the expense is necessary for the company, whether there is a better way, and whether they can explain it to their colleagues. By eliminating detailed rules, the responsibility of judgment actually becomes heavier.

This is the slightly counterintuitive part of "No Rules." It seems like rules are eliminated, but in reality, purpose is intensely shared. The more you reduce detailed regulations, the clearer you must be about what you are aiming for, what you value, and what level of risk you are willing to tolerate.

"No Rules" does not mean the absence of control. It is a transition from controlling behavior through detailed regulations to aligning behavior through context and human judgment.

## Three Assumptions That Support Freedom

The prerequisites Netflix emphasized for letting go of rules are talent density, candor, and context.

Talent density is not just about gathering highly skilled people. It refers to a state where there are enough people in the organization who can understand the purpose, make decisions in uncertain situations, and explain their reasoning. It also includes the ability to collaborate with others, accept opposing views, and learn from mistakes.

It is dangerous to understand talent density solely as "eliminating low performers." If a culture of hiding mistakes and viewing each other as competitors emerges, it will destroy the candor required next. We need a perspective that nurtures capability not just through hiring, but through information sharing, learning opportunities, role design, and decision-making experience.

Candor serves as the mechanism to correct free judgments. If there are detailed rules, you can check "did they follow the procedure?" If you reduce procedures, you must be able to point out to each other whether a decision truly aligns with the purpose.

Candor is not the same as saying whatever comes to mind. It addresses actions and decisions rather than personality, provides evidence, and is delivered in a way that helps the other person improve. If employees cannot voice dissent to their managers, substantive control remains even if authority is decentralized.

The third element, context, includes the organization's purpose, priorities, budget, risks, past failures, and legal or ethical constraints. Telling employees to "make your own decisions" while managers hold onto all the information is not delegation; it is closer to abandonment.

The role of managers shifts from approving every action to providing the materials necessary for good judgment. Netflix's mantra, "Context, not Control," represents this shift.

## Who Makes the Final Call?

Handing authority to the front lines often creates a different problem. While trying to listen to everyone's opinion, no one can make a decision. Consensus-building takes too much time, and responsibility for failure becomes ambiguous.

At Netflix, an "Informed Captain" is designated for each important decision. Rather than a manager deciding simply because of their rank, the person leading the project becomes the decision-maker.

An Informed Captain is not someone who decides arbitrarily. They gather necessary information, listen to experts and stakeholders, and actively seek out dissenting opinions. With all that in hand, they make the final call and take ownership of the outcome.

This mechanism lies between top-down centralization and unanimous consensus. It decentralizes decision-making to the front lines without blurring "who made the decision."

Freedom is not about acting however you like. It is about being able to use your own judgment within a shared purpose and context. Responsibility, too, is not about punishing individuals who fail. It is about listening to necessary opinions, explaining your reasoning, checking the results, and changing the next decision if there was an error. It means taking ownership of this entire process.

Freedom without context is abandonment. Responsibility without authority is passing the buck. To make freedom and responsibility work, you must hand over information, authority, and resources together.

## Do Not Import Netflix's Systems As-Is

Reading about Netflix might make you want to eliminate vacation policies and approval workflows immediately. However, copying only the surface of these systems is dangerous.

Erin Meyer argues that how people voice disagreement to managers or deliver negative feedback varies by country and region. While Japanese organizations vary widely, there is a general tendency to find it difficult to disagree directly with managers, to express negative opinions indirectly, and to value consensus over individual decisions.

In such an environment, suddenly declaring "let's speak candidly" or "let's make our own decisions" is unlikely to work well. Candor can turn into aggressive language, and delegation can turn into managers abandoning their responsibilities.

What is needed is not Netflix's specific systems, but transferring the conditions that make those systems work. If it is hard to disagree in public, allow dissent to be raised in 1-on-1s or in writing. If you delegate decisions, clearly specify the decision-maker and their advisors. If you reduce expense approvals, share the company's financial situation and decision criteria beforehand. Start with a small scope so you can roll back if problems arise.

Adapting to culture does not mean giving up on change. It means finding a way for candor and delegation to function within your own organization. Here, too, the concept of adaptive institutional design is highly applicable.

## What Lies Beyond the Rules?

There is no need to let go of all rules. Clear control is necessary for safety, compliance, information security, prevention of discrimination and harassment, and safeguarding rights. We should not leave things to individual discretion in areas where variance in judgment can cause severe harm.

The question to ask is not whether there are too many or too few rules. It is whether control or creation is needed for the problem at hand. Do we need detailed procedures, or are principles and judgment enough? Do the assumptions underlying our current rules still hold true?

Good rules are not rules that are followed forever. They are rules that people create as needed, review according to circumstances, and let go of once they have served their purpose.

Beyond following the rules, do human judgment, growth, trust, and collaboration remain? If all that remains is compliance with the rules, then it is time to redefine our relationship with our tools.
