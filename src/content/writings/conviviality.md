## Designing Products We Can Choose, Reshape, and Let Go Of

![Neumorphism illustration depicting a person choosing, combining, and letting go of multiple tools](/images/writings/hero-neumorphism.png)

Have you ever felt like you were being "used by your tools"?

By "tools," I do not just mean physical objects like cars or keyboards. It also includes the technologies and institutions that humans have created to improve our lives and work. Smartphones, social media, AI, schools, healthcare, laws, and organizational rules are all, in a broad sense, tools created by humans.

I once felt "used by my tools" while programming with generative AI.

At the time, every time an error occurred, I would paste the code into the AI, try the suggested fix, and if another error popped up, ask the AI again. My original goal in using AI was to resolve the error. Yet, before I knew it, keeping up the conversation with the AI had become the core activity itself.

After a few hours, I closed the AI, checked the error message and the execution flow myself, and resolved the issue in just a few minutes.

Of course, I cannot conclude from this single experience that "we shouldn't use AI." Today's AI is incredibly powerful, capable of analyzing entire codebases and proposing fixes across multiple files. I still use AI on a daily basis myself.

The problem was not that I relied on AI. The problem was that I lost sight of my goal—solving the issue—and fell into an automatic loop of "asking the AI next." I thought I was using the AI, but in reality, I had unconsciously surrendered my own thinking.

This inversion of means and ends happens with things other than AI as well.

- Opening social media to check a message, only to mindlessly scroll through recommended posts.
- Buying unnecessary products just to earn reward points.
- A manual designed to solve customer problems becoming an end in itself, where following the manual perfectly is all that matters.
- Business chat tools meant to facilitate smooth work demanding constant, instant replies, robbing us of time for deep, focused thought.

Tools were created to expand our capabilities and make us freer. However, when using the tool itself becomes the goal, or when other options are lost, the tool ends up restricting our behavior instead.

So, what should product creators consider to prevent more people from being used by their tools?

In this article, we will explore this question using the concept of "conviviality" proposed by philosopher Ivan Illich, alongside its modern interpretation by design engineer Hisato Ogata.

The core message of this article is this:

> A good tool is not one that traps its users, but one that they can choose, reshape, and, if necessary, let go of.

## What Does It Mean to Be "Used by Our Tools"?

The phrase "being used by our tools" is convenient but ambiguous. Frequently using a tool or relying on it does not immediately mean we are being "used" by it.

We live in dependence on various tools and systems, such as healthcare, education, transportation, electricity, and the internet. We let alarm clocks wake us up and map apps guide our way. This kind of dependence can actually help us lead our lives autonomously.

The problem is not dependence itself, but losing the ability to choose our relationship with the tool. In this article, we define "being used by our tools" as a state in which the following three elements are lost:

![Three states of being used by tools. By losing purpose, options, and agency, one can no longer choose their relationship with the tool.](/images/writings/three-losses.svg)

*Figure 1: Three states of being used by tools*

### Losing Purpose

This is a state where continuing to use the tool takes precedence over the original purpose for which you started using it.

You were supposed to be using AI to resolve an error, but you end up running in circles around the AI's output, unable to solve the issue. You opened social media to contact someone, but you keep scrolling through the feed. In these moments, the tool is no longer a means to an end; it has become the end itself.

### Losing Options

This is a state where you cannot switch to other methods, making the use of a single tool practically mandatory.

For example, in areas designed entirely around cars, walking or using public transit may no longer be realistic options. If all workplace information is stored in a specific service, it becomes difficult for an individual to decide not to use that service.

This cannot be solved by individual willpower or self-management alone, because the option of *not* using the tool has been designed out of the society or organization.

### Losing Agency

This is a state where, instead of thinking and operating the tool yourself, you act automatically in response to notifications, recommendations, ratings, or procedures presented by the tool.

Recommendations and automation are not inherently bad. The issue is whether you can reject those recommendations, modify how the tool behaves, or verify why a certain result was produced. If humans cannot participate in the final decision, we risk trading our agency for convenience.

Product creators must consider whether users are losing these three elements.

Retention rates, screen time, post counts, and purchase frequencies are important metrics for improving a product. However, when these numbers become the ultimate goal, there is a danger of optimizing for "how long we can keep them hooked" rather than "whether the user's goal was achieved."

A user continuing to use a product is not necessarily the same thing as that user being liberated by it.

## Illich's "Two Watersheds"

The philosophy of Ivan Illich provides a helpful clue for thinking about this issue.

Illich was a 20th-century philosopher and social critic who critiqued industrial society through the lens of institutions like schools, hospitals, and transportation. His representative works include *Deschooling Society* and *Tools for Conviviality*.

Illich did not limit the word "technology" to machines and physical products. He viewed institutions that shape human behavior and relationships—such as schools, healthcare, transportation, and laws—as "tools" as well.

He argued that the development of tools and institutions passes through "two watersheds."

In the process of crossing the first watershed, the development of tools and institutions significantly improves human capabilities and lives. Medicine cures more diseases, schools grant more people access to knowledge, and transportation allows people to travel long distances.

However, when tools and institutions expand without limit and cross the second watershed, they begin to obstruct their original purpose.

A healthcare system designed to support health creates a state where people cannot speak about their own well-being without a specialist's diagnosis. A school system designed for learning stops recognizing the value of learning that happens outside of school. Cars, meant to make travel convenient, create cities where it is impossible to move around without a car.

What is happening here is not simply that the tools have flaws. Rather, as tools overdevelop, the means encroach upon the ends, resulting in an inversion where the tool actually exacerbates the very problem it was meant to solve.

![A curve diagram showing how the expansion of tools and institutions initially increases capability and freedom, but obstructs the original purpose after crossing the second watershed.](/images/writings/two-watersheds.svg)

*Figure 2: The development of tools and the two watersheds*

We cannot directly apply this concept to an individual's use of AI, as Illich primarily dealt with industries and institutions that encompass society as a whole.

Even so, the two watersheds offer a valuable perspective for distinguishing between the stage where AI expands our capabilities and the stage where we become unable to think or make decisions without it.

There is a difference between AI helping us understand code and us becoming unable to read code without an AI's explanation. There is a difference between AI expanding our options and us only considering the options presented by AI.

The question we must ask is not "Are we using AI?" but "Does using AI increase the choices available to humans?"

## What Is a Convivial Tool?

As the antithesis to industrial tools, Illich envisioned "convivial tools."

"Conviviality" is sometimes translated into Japanese as *jiritsu kyōsei* (autonomous coexistence). This does not simply mean living in harmony with others, nature, or technology.

What Illich emphasized was that humans, while existing in mutual interdependence with others, can still choose their own purposes and act creatively. Rather than aiming for isolated individuals who depend on no one, the key is to maintain one's own freedom and the freedom of others within those relationships of dependence.

A convivial tool does not impose a specific purpose on its user; instead, it can be used for purposes the user chooses for themselves. Furthermore, the power to use or modify it is not monopolized by a handful of specialists; it is accessible to many, allowing them to utilize it in their own ways. It is also crucial that one person's use of the tool does not deprive others of their freedom to use it in the same way.

In other words, a convivial tool is not one that decides everything on behalf of humans, but one that expands the space for humans to think, express themselves, and build relationships with others.

In the context of modern digital products, this manifests in qualities such as:

- Users being able to modify how notifications and recommendations behave.
- Being able to export data and use it in other services.
- Being able to expand use cases through APIs and extensions.
- Users being able to decide the scope of automation themselves.
- Being able to reject or modify AI suggestions and return to one's own judgment.

![Comparison between enclosing tools that prevent modification or departure, and tools that expand freedom to choose, reshape, and migrate.](/images/writings/enclosure-vs-convivial.svg)

*Figure 3: Enclosing tools vs. tools that expand freedom*

However, having many settings or open-sourcing the code does not automatically make a tool convivial. If the people who can actually use those features are limited, or if modifying them requires highly specialized knowledge, power remains concentrated in the hands of a few.

What matters is not the number of features, but how much agency the user has in shaping their relationship with the tool.

## Use, Make, Let Go

Design engineer Hisato Ogata, author of *Convivial Technology*, uses Illich's philosophy as a guide to examine the relationship between humans and modern technology.

An important perspective that emerges from Ogata's discussion is that we are not merely passive "users" of tools. We can also make tools, reshape them, and let go of them when they are no longer needed.

![A cycle diagram centered on human purpose, showing the loop of using, reshaping, letting go of, and re-choosing tools.](/images/writings/use-make-let-go.svg)

*Figure 4: The cycle of use, make, and let go*

### Use: Understanding Purpose and Limits

To use a tool autonomously, we must understand what purpose it was designed for, what it can do, and what it cannot do.

What we must watch out for here is the existence of multiple, competing purposes within a single product.

A user might use social media to stay in touch with friends. Meanwhile, the company providing the service might be trying to maximize screen time to display ads. A user might use AI to write code efficiently, but the AI is not necessarily designed to deepen the user's understanding.

Using a tool is not just about learning how to operate it. It is also about understanding what goals and metrics drive the tool, and judging whether they align with your own purposes.

Creators have a responsibility to help users understand not just the features a product offers, but also its limitations, uncertainties, and what it is optimized for.

### Make: From Consumers to Participants

In modern society, we are often treated merely as "users" who purchase finished tools and use them in predetermined ways.

In product development, creators research user problems and design and deliver optimal solutions. While this is an important endeavor, when creators decide everything, they can end up shutting out the possibility for users to reshape tools to fit their own unique situations.

By "making," I do not mean that every user must write code or manufacture products from scratch.

Changing settings. Turning off notifications. Choosing display items. Editing templates. Adding extensions. Connecting to other services via APIs. Participating in the formation of operational rules. All of these are acts of reshaping tools to align with our own purposes.

Generative AI is expanding this potential even further. Even those without specialized knowledge in programming or design are becoming able to create small, personalized tools using natural language.

On the other hand, if the creation environment itself depends entirely on a few AI companies, we may lose the freedom to choose or modify the underlying foundation, even if we can create things freely on the surface.

We must consider not only what we can make, but also who controls the means of making it.

### Let Go: Preserving the Freedom Not to Use

Tools are not made for the purpose of being used indefinitely. They are made for humans to achieve a specific goal.

After cutting wood with a handsaw, we do not keep holding onto the saw. We put it down, and pick it up again when needed. Letting go does not mean throwing it away or denying its value. It means maintaining a relationship where we use it when necessary and can step away when it is not.

In digital products, "letting go" can take many forms:

- Temporarily pausing use.
- Returning to doing things manually.
- Migrating data to another service.
- Disabling only specific features.
- "Graduating" from the service once the goal is achieved.

Of course, continuous use is not inherently bad. Some products, like calendars, accounting software, communication tools, and infrastructure, fulfill their purpose precisely through continuous use.

What we must distinguish is whether users continue to use a product because it is valuable, or because they are unable to leave.

Creators naturally want users to keep using their products. It is necessary to sustain a business, and it is a natural human desire to want what you built to be used for a long time.

However, when screen time or retention rates are prioritized over the user's actual goals, the product shifts from a tool that helps people to a mechanism that traps them.

A good product is not just one that is used forever. It is a product that users can choose to keep using, or choose to leave for another alternative.

## What Product Creators Should Consider

Convivial tools cannot be automatically created from a specific set of UI patterns or feature checklists. Illich himself did not view conviviality as a prescription to be mechanically applied, but as a set of criteria for society to protect its freedom.

When building a product, we can, at the very least, ask ourselves the following questions:

- Whose purpose is this product optimizing for?
- Are we confusing increased usage time with the achievement of the user's goals?
- Can users reject or modify recommendations, automation, and notifications?
- Is there a way left for users *not* to use the product, or to migrate to another product?
- Can users export their own data?
- Can people without specialized knowledge participate in shaping how the tool works?
- Does making things convenient unnecessarily strip users of their own judgment or skills?
- Does the convenience of some people deprive others of their options?

There is not always a single correct answer to these questions.

There are times when user modifications must be restricted to ensure safety. Sometimes, reducing complex settings makes a tool more accessible to a wider audience. In highly public systems, we need fairness and accountability, not just individual freedom.

This is why conviviality is not a simple design principle like "just make it freely customizable" or "just let them quit."

It is a perspective for continuously questioning who decides the balance, and how, between autonomy and heteronomy, freedom and safety, individual choice and the choices of others, convenience and creativity.

## Future Outlook: Thinking About Rules and AI

Illich wrote *Tools for Conviviality* in the 1970s. Since then, computers and the internet have become ubiquitous, and today, AI interprets human intent, makes proposals, and sometimes acts autonomously.

As the boundary between tools and humans shifts, the perspective of conviviality is more important than ever.

In future articles, I will connect this discussion to "rules" and "AI."

Rules are not just for controlling people. They can also be tools that clarify roles and expectations, allowing people to collaborate with peace of mind. On the other hand, when following the rules becomes an end in itself, it robs people on the ground of judgment and dialogue.

How can we design rules that enable people to collaborate autonomously, rather than rules that simply demand compliance? We will examine whether people can participate in the rule-making process, object to rules, modify them according to the situation, and let go of them when necessary.

Unlike traditional tools, AI does not just return a predetermined output for a given human input. It can interpret ambiguous intent, make plans, operate external systems, and act while interacting with humans.

How can humans maintain their purpose and agency in the face of such AI? Where in the process of AI's intent comprehension, planning, execution, and presentation of results should we leave room for humans to verify, modify, or reject?

AI has the potential to allow people who could never build tools before to create the tools they need. At the same time, there is a risk of over-relying on AI outputs, becoming unable to make decisions without AI, and falling into a state of thinking only within the options presented by AI.

It is not a binary choice of using AI or not. What matters is choosing which AI to use, for what purpose, and to what extent—modifying how we use it as needed, and always being able to return to human judgment.

We cannot live without tools. No human is completely autonomous, independent of everyone and everything.

That is why what we need is not to eliminate dependence, but to be able to choose what we depend on. It is about not being locked into a single tool, having multiple options, participating in how tools are shaped, and being able to let go of them when necessary.

The purpose of a product is not to keep people using it indefinitely. It is to enable people to achieve their own goals, and to think, make, and connect with others more freely.

Perhaps a good tool is not one that lives on behalf of humans, but one that expands the space for humans to shape their own lives.
