## Designing Products We Can Choose, Modify, and Let Go Of

![A Neumorphism illustration representing a person choosing, reconfiguring, and letting go of multiple tools](/images/writings/hero-neumorphism.png)

Have you ever felt like you were "being used by your tools"?

By "tools," I do not just mean physical objects like cars or keyboards. It also includes the technologies and institutions humans have created to improve their lives and work. Smartphones, social media, AI, schools, healthcare, laws, and organizational rules are all, in a broad sense, tools made by humans.

I once felt like I was "being used by my tools" when I was programming using generative AI.

At the time, every time an error occurred, I would paste the code into the AI, try the suggested fix, and if another error popped up, ask the AI again. The original purpose of using AI was supposed to be resolving the error. Yet, before I knew it, continuing the conversation with the AI had become the core of my work.

After a few hours, I closed the AI, checked the error message and the flow of execution myself, and resolved the problem in just a few minutes.

Of course, I cannot say "it is better not to use AI" based solely on this experience. Today's AI is incredibly powerful, capable of analyzing codebases and proposing fixes across multiple files. I still use AI on a daily basis myself.

The problem was not that I relied on AI. It was that I had lost sight of the goal—solving the problem—and was automatically repeating the action of "asking the AI next." I thought I was using the AI, but without realizing it, I had abandoned thinking for myself.

This inversion of ends and means happens with things other than AI as well.

- Opening social media to check a message, only to mindlessly scroll through recommended posts.
- Making earning reward points the goal, leading to buying products you don't even need.
- A manual designed to solve customer problems turning into an end in itself, where following the manual becomes the sole objective.
- Business chat tools meant to facilitate smooth work demanding constant instant replies, robbing us of time for deep, focused thinking.

Tools have been created to expand our capabilities and make us freer. However, when using the tool itself becomes the goal, or when other options are lost, the tool ends up restricting our behavior instead.

So, what should product creators consider to prevent more people from being used by their tools?

In this article, we will explore this question using the concept of "conviviality" proposed by philosopher Ivan Illich, alongside its modern interpretation by design engineer Hisato Ogata.

What I want to convey in this article is this:

> A good tool is not something that traps people, but something that users can choose, modify, and let go of when necessary.

## What Does It Mean to Be Used by a Tool?

The phrase "being used by a tool" is convenient but ambiguous. Frequently using a tool or depending on it does not immediately mean you are being "used" by it.

We live in dependence on various tools and systems, such as healthcare, education, transportation, electricity, and the internet. We let alarm clocks wake us up and map apps guide our way. This kind of dependence can actually help us lead our lives autonomously.

The problem is not the dependence itself, but the inability to choose our relationship with the tool. In this article, we define "being used by a tool" as a state in which the following three things are lost:

![Three states of being used by tools. By losing purpose, options, and agency, one becomes unable to choose their relationship with the tool.](/images/writings/three-losses.svg)

*Figure 1: Three states of being used by tools*

### Losing Purpose

This is a state where continuing to use the tool takes priority over the original purpose for which you started using it.

You were supposed to be using AI to resolve an error, but you end up flustered by the AI's output and fail to solve the issue. You were supposed to open social media to contact someone, but you keep scrolling through the screen. In these moments, the tool is no longer a means to achieve a goal; it has become the goal itself.

### Losing Options

This is a state where you cannot switch to other methods, making the use of a single tool virtually mandatory.

For example, in areas designed around cars, walking or using public transit may no longer be realistic options. If all workplace information is accumulated in a specific service, it is difficult for an individual to decide not to use that service.

This cannot be solved by individual willpower or self-management alone, because the option of *not* using the tool has been designed out of society or the organization.

### Losing Agency

This is a state where, instead of thinking and operating the tool yourself, you act automatically in accordance with the notifications, recommendations, ratings, or procedures presented by the tool.

Recommendations and automation themselves are not inherently bad. The issue is whether you can reject those recommendations, modify how they work, or verify why a certain result was produced. If humans cannot participate in the final decision-making, we risk trading away our agency for convenience.

Product creators need to consider whether users are losing these things.

Retention rate, usage time, post count, and purchase frequency are important metrics for improving a product. However, when those numbers become the ultimate goal, there is a danger of optimizing for "how long we can keep them using it" rather than "whether the user's purpose was achieved."

A user continuing to use a product is not necessarily the same thing as them being liberated by that product.

## Ivan Illich's "Two Watersheds"

Ivan Illich's philosophy provides a clue to thinking about this problem.

Illich was a 20th-century philosopher and social critic who critiqued industrial society through the lens of institutions like schools, hospitals, and transportation. His representative works include *Deschooling Society* and *Tools for Conviviality*.

Illich did not limit the word "technology" to machines and products. He viewed institutions that shape human behavior and relationships—such as schools, healthcare, transportation, and laws—as "tools" as well.

He argued that the development of tools and institutions has "two watersheds."

In the process of crossing the first watershed, the development of tools and institutions significantly improves human capabilities and lives. Medical care cures more diseases, schools allow more people to access knowledge, and means of transportation enable people to travel far.

However, when tools and institutions expand without limit and cross the second watershed, they begin to obstruct their original purpose.

A healthcare system meant to support health creates a state where people cannot speak about their own health without a specialist's diagnosis. A school system meant for learning stops recognizing the value of learning that happens outside of school. Cars meant to make travel convenient create cities where you cannot travel without a car.

What is happening here is not simply that the tools have flaws. It is an inversion where, because tools develop too far, the means encroach upon the ends, actually exacerbating the very problems the tools were meant to solve.

![A curve diagram showing how the expansion of tools and institutions increases capability and freedom, but obstructs the original purpose after crossing the second watershed](/images/writings/two-watersheds.svg)

*Figure 2: The development of tools and the two watersheds*

This concept cannot be directly applied to an individual's use of AI, as Illich primarily dealt with industries and institutions that encompass society as a whole.

Still, the two watersheds offer an important perspective for distinguishing between the stage where AI expands our capabilities and the stage where we can no longer think or make decisions without AI.

There is a difference between AI helping you understand code and you being unable to read code without an AI's explanation. There is a difference between AI expanding your options and you only considering the options presented by AI.

The question we must ask is not "Are we using AI?" but "Does using AI increase the choices humans can make?"

## What Is a Convivial Tool?

Illich envisioned "convivial tools" as the antithesis of industrial tools.

Conviviality is sometimes translated into Japanese as "jiritsu kyōsei" (autonomous coexistence). This does not simply mean living in harmony with others, nature, or technology.

What Illich emphasized was that humans, while existing in mutual interdependence with others, can choose their own purposes and act creatively. Rather than aiming to be isolated individuals who depend on no one, what matters is maintaining our own freedom and the freedom of others within those relationships of dependence.

Convivial tools do not impose a specific purpose on users; instead, they can be used for purposes chosen by the users themselves. Furthermore, the authority to use or modify them is not monopolized by a handful of specialists; many people can access and utilize them in their own ways. It is also crucial that one person's use of a tool does not deprive others of the freedom to use it in the same way.

In other words, a convivial tool is not one that decides everything on behalf of humans, but one that expands the space for humans to think, express themselves, and build relationships with others.

In the context of modern digital products, this manifests in qualities such as:

- Users being able to modify the behavior of notifications and recommendations.
- Being able to export data and use it in other services.
- Being able to expand use cases through APIs and extensions.
- Users being able to decide the scope of automation themselves.
- Being able to reject or modify AI suggestions and return to one's own judgment.

![A comparison between enclosing tools that do not allow modification or departure, and tools that expand freedom to choose, modify, and move](/images/writings/enclosure-vs-convivial.svg)

*Figure 3: Enclosing tools vs. tools that expand freedom*

However, having many settings or open-sourcing the code does not automatically make a tool convivial. If the people who can actually use it are limited, or if modifying it requires highly specialized knowledge, authority remains concentrated in the hands of a few.

What matters is not the number of features, but how much the user can engage with and shape their relationship with the tool.

## Use, Make, Let Go

Design engineer Hisato Ogata, author of *Convivial Technology*, uses Illich's philosophy as a guide to examine the relationship between humans and modern technology.

An important perspective that emerges from Ogata's discussion is that we are not beings who merely "use" tools. We can also make tools, modify them, and let them go when they are no longer needed.

![A cycle diagram centered on human purpose, showing the process of using, modifying, letting go of, and re-choosing tools](/images/writings/use-make-let-go.svg)

*Figure 4: The cycle of using, making, and letting go*

### Use: Knowing the Purpose and Limits

To use a tool autonomously, we need to understand what purpose it was designed for, what it can do, and what it cannot do.

What we must watch out for here is that multiple purposes can coexist within a single product.

A user might use social media to stay in touch with friends. Meanwhile, the company providing the service might be trying to maximize screen time to display ads. A user uses AI to write code efficiently, but the AI is not necessarily designed to deepen the user's understanding.

Using a tool is not just about learning how to operate it. It is also about understanding what purposes or metrics drive the tool, and judging whether they align with your own goals.

Creators have a responsibility to help users understand not just the features a product offers, but also its limitations, uncertainties, and what it is optimized for.

### Make: From Consumers to Participants

In modern society, we are often treated as "users" who purchase finished tools and use them in predetermined ways.

In product development as well, creators research user problems and design and deliver optimal solutions. While this is an important endeavor, if creators decide everything, they can shut out the possibility of users modifying the tools to suit their own situations.

By "making," I do not mean that every user must write code or manufacture products from scratch.

Changing settings. Turning off notifications. Choosing display items. Editing templates. Adding extensions. Connecting to other services using APIs. Participating in shaping operational rules. All of these are acts of modifying tools to fit our own purposes.

Generative AI is expanding this possibility even further. Even those who previously lacked specialized knowledge in programming or design are becoming able to build the small tools they need through natural language.

On the other hand, if the creation environment itself depends on a handful of AI companies, we might lose the freedom to choose or modify the underlying foundation, even if we can build things freely on the surface.

We must consider not only what can be made, but also who controls the means of making it.

### Let Go: Preserving the Freedom Not to Use

Tools are not made for the purpose of being used indefinitely. They are made for humans to achieve some purpose.

After cutting wood with a saw, we do not keep holding onto the saw. We set it down, and use it again when it is needed. Letting go is not about discarding or denying the value of a tool. It is about maintaining a relationship where we can use it when necessary and step away when it is not.

"Letting go" in digital products takes various forms:

- Temporarily stopping use.
- Returning to doing things manually.
- Moving to another service with your data.
- Disabling only certain features.
- Graduating from the service after achieving your goal.

Of course, continuous use itself is not bad. Some products, like calendars, accounting software, communications, and infrastructure, fulfill their purpose through continuous use.

What we must distinguish is whether we continue to use a tool because it is valuable, or because we cannot leave it.

Creators want users to continue using their products. It is necessary to sustain a business, and the desire to have what you made used for a long time is a natural human feeling.

However, if usage time or retention rates are prioritized over the user's purpose, the product shifts from a tool that helps people to a mechanism that traps them.

A good product is not just one that is used forever. It is a product that users can choose to keep using of their own free will, or move away from to another method.

## What Product Creators Should Consider

Convivial tools cannot be automatically created from a specific set of UI patterns or a checklist of features. Illich himself did not view conviviality as a prescription to be mechanically applied, but as a set of criteria for society to continuously protect its freedom.

When building a product, we can at least ask ourselves the following questions:

- Whose purpose is this product optimizing for?
- Are we confusing increased usage time with the achievement of the user's goals?
- Can users reject or modify recommendations, automation, notifications, etc.?
- Is there a way left for users not to use the product, or to move to another product?
- Can users export their own data?
- Can people without specialized knowledge participate in shaping how the tool should be?
- By making things convenient, are we unnecessarily stripping away the user's own judgment or skills?
- Does the convenience of some people deprive others of their options?

There is not always a single correct answer to these questions.

There are times when user modifications must be restricted to protect safety. Sometimes, reducing complex settings makes a tool easier for more people to use. In highly public systems, we need fairness and accountability, not just individual freedom.

That is why conviviality is not a simple design principle like "it's fine as long as it can be freely modified" or "it's fine as long as users can stop using it."

It is a perspective for continuously questioning who decides the balance, and how, between autonomy and heteronomy, freedom and safety, individual choice and the choices of others, convenience and creativity.

## Future Outlook: Thinking About Rules and AI

Illich wrote *Tools for Conviviality* in the 1970s. Since then, computers and the internet have spread, and today AI interprets human intent, makes suggestions, and sometimes acts autonomously.

Now, as the boundary between tools and humans shifts, the perspective of conviviality is more important than ever.

In future articles, I will connect this discussion to "rules" and "AI."

Rules are not just for controlling people. They can also be tools to clarify roles and expectations, helping people collaborate with peace of mind. On the other hand, if following the rules becomes the goal itself, it robs people of on-the-ground judgment and dialogue.

How can we design rules for people to collaborate autonomously, rather than rules to make people comply? We will examine whether people can participate in the rule-making process, object to them, modify them according to the situation, and let them go if necessary.

Unlike conventional tools, AI does not just return a predetermined response to human input. It can interpret ambiguous intent, make plans, operate external systems, and act while interacting with humans.

How can humans maintain their purpose and agency in the face of such AI? Where in the process of AI's intent comprehension, planning, execution, and presentation of results should we leave room for humans to verify, modify, or reject?

AI has the potential to allow people who could never make tools before to build the tools they need. On the other hand, there is a risk of over-relying on AI outputs, becoming unable to make decisions without AI, and falling into a state of thinking only within the options presented by AI.

It is not a binary choice of using AI or not. What is important is choosing which AI to use, for what purpose, and to what extent, modifying how we use it as needed, and being able to return to human judgment at any time.

We cannot live without tools. There is no human who is completely autonomous and depends on no one and nothing.

That is why what we need is not to eliminate dependence, but to be able to choose what we depend on. It is about not being locked into a single tool, having multiple options, participating in how tools should be, and being able to let them go if necessary.

The purpose of a product is not to keep people using it. It is to enable people to realize their own goals, think and make more freely, and engage with others.

Perhaps a good tool is not one that lives on behalf of humans, but one that expands the space for humans to shape their own lives.

## Finally: Six Questions

Finally, let us ask ourselves the "six questions" presented by Hisato Ogata, keeping in mind the rules, systems, services, or apps we are currently building.

Rather than trying to find immediate answers, the act of continuously asking these questions will be the first step toward design that protects the user's autonomy and choices.

>- Does that technology strip humans of their ability to survive in the natural environment?
>- Does that technology create an irreplaceable monopoly, making humans dependent on it?
>- Does that technology manipulate humans to act according to its program, causing them to stop thinking?
>- Does that technology create polarization and disparity between those who manipulate and those who are manipulated?
>- Does that technology merely obsolesce the value of existing things at an excessive speed?
>- Do we feel frustration or a sense of unease toward that technology?   
>Hisato Ogata, *Convivial Technology: Toward a Society Where Humans and Technology Coexist*, Chapter 6, "Toward Convivial Technology"

In this article, we have broadly referred to rules, systems, services, and apps as "tools," not just physical products. Therefore, you can replace the word "technology" here with the "tools" you are creating.
